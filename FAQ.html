Frequently Asked Questions

Essential answers on Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) and how to prepare for floods, earthquakes, landslides, and droughts.

General Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR)

The definition of Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR)is a process where there is identification, assessment, and reduction of risk of disasters that may arise from natural hazards. This is not about dealing with the impact after a disaster but more of prevention of such disasters from happening in the first place.

The emergency pack must be sufficient enough to last your family for at least three days. Essential items include:
  • Drinking water that is clean (about 3-4 liters per person) and non-perishable food.
  • First aid kit, necessary prescriptions, and hygiene items.
  • Flashlight and extra batteries, whistle, and a radio that can run on battery power or by hand-crank mechanism.
  • Important papers in a waterproof case.
  • Small denomination cash, power bank, and a multi-tool.

Select two secure meeting places—one immediately outside your home in case of emergencies such as earthquakes and another one farther away from your neighborhood in case you need to evacuate. Select an out-of-town family member or a friend whom you can rely on for communication, since distant calls may pass through in case of network overload.

Flood Preparedness & Safety

Make sure that storm drains and local waterways are not littered with any waste materials. Raise your electrical panels, appliances, and other valuable possessions to an elevation level above the flood water level. Install check valves on your plumbing system and save your local wetlands.

6 inches (15 cm)of fast-moving water is enough to sweep a person off his/her feet, while 12 inches (30 cm)of flowing water has enough power to move almost any vehicle. The other dangers associated with floodwater include hidden roadways, exposed sewer drains, sharp debris, power line threats, and pollutants.

Earthquake Preparedness & Safety

Follow "Drop, Cover, and Hold On":
  • DROP to your hands and knees to avoid being thrown off balance by the earthquake.
  • COVER yourself by holding on to your head and neck beneath a desk, table, or corner of an interior wall.
  • HOLD ON until the shaking stops. Do not go outside or use doorways during the earthquake.

Secure all tall bookshelves, wardrobes, water tanks, and heavy appliances to wall studs. Store all heavy and fragile objects on bottom shelves and install locks on your kitchen cabinets. Learn how to turn off all major gas, water, and electrical sources inside your home.

Landslide Awareness & Prevention

Look for fresh fissures or bulges on the surface of the earth, paved roads, and building foundations; sudden jamming of doors and windows; tilted trees, fences, and utility poles; changes in the water flow in streams or muddy water in streams; and low rumbles or cracking noises from the movement of trees and rocks uphill.

Avoid removing the deep-rooted trees and plants that hold the soil particles together on steep slopes. Construct proper systems of draining to ensure that rain water does not flow over the slopes. Avoid construction activities at the foot or top part of any steep slope.

Drought Resilience & Water Management

Unlike floods and earthquakes, droughts emerge slowly through months and years by draining the soil moisture and reservoirs. This means that preparation for drought entails water conservation, water collection, protection of water catchment areas, and planting of drought-resistant plants.

Fix any leaks from faucets and toilets right away, use low flow shower heads and aerators, harvest rainwater for other than drinking purposes, recycle graywater such as fruit and vegetable washing for watering plants, and irrigate gardens in the early mornings or evenings to reduce evaporation.
